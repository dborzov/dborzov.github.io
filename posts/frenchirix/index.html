Frenchirix and the Research on How We Learn Languages

TL;DR: What academic research on language learning tells us:

  • Like learning to bike. Language is acquired subconsciously, by brain machinery built for it. Studying things like grammar matters, but its value is like that of a physics explanation when you’re learning to ride a bike: it helps you know what to notice. But the bulk of the work is getting back on that bike over and over, until you start falling a bit less.
  • Input before output. That brain machinery activates most easily when you hear or read something in the target language that you can at least partially understand. So getting more exposure to it, under any pretext, is where it’s at. Speaking and writing grow out of that, so don’t rush them, and don’t keep testing.
  • Words map to the world. The instinct we’re tapping into is for understanding people, not for passing conjugation quizzes.
  • Stories make it real. The brain learns from messages it cares about. Example sentences that mean nothing mostly teach boredom.
  • It’s okay to be confused. Guessing from context is how acquisition works. Don’t stop to dissect every sentence.
  • Use the real thing. Learn the French people actually speak, not textbook French. Kids’ TV shows are excellent material.

And what I did with it: Frenchirix, a small app for spaced repetition of dialogue from an unadapted French TV show. Its code is open source.

Introduction

My kids are in French immersion. They are awesome and generally ace the hell out of that French, but I’ve also watched them run into the same potholes I hit as a kid.

It made me think back to my childhood years of learning English in school. If you added up all the hours of “instruction and practice” I sat through, you would expect me to be able to read Shakespeare with ease.

But, as you can guess, the reality was less impressive. Looking back, I realize that a lot of those hours ended up being pointless busywork.

To be fair, my kids have it much better. We’ve seen some of the teachers at their school go out of their way to do an incredible job for their students. Our family is very grateful to them for it.

But individual teachers aside, I got the impression that the way schools approach language learning is still the same rigid system I remember.

For some reason, that got to me. As a parent, the last thing you want is for your kids to go through the same thing you did. You want them to skip the bad parts.

So I started wondering if there was anything useful I could do about it.

But as I kept thinking about it, I was surprised to realize I didn’t really know how.

This was odd, because I’d ended up having to learn two languages as an adult. In theory, this experience alone should have taught me what works and what doesn’t.

But if anything, the main thing that experience had taught me was distrust.

Language learning is full of traps. Some kinds of work look exactly like what studying is supposed to look like, yet much of the effort spent on them is wasted.

In most things, the more experience you have, the more confident you get. Language learning was different. I was surest of how it worked at the start, before I’d tried anything. Then I spent years at it, and what I mostly came away with was a deep respect for how much more there is to it than meets the eye.

Yes, through trial and error I stumbled on a few tricks that worked well enough to get me by. But why those? Would they work for anyone else? If I was honest with myself, I didn’t know.

After all those years, it was still alchemy to me: mysterious, murky, nothing quite what it seemed.

Then I realized the way out was the same as it was for alchemy: chemistry.

So I went looking for what’s actually known: findings backed by evidence and studies, not opinions and impressions.

I figured that with all these AI tools, I would be able to do it right this time. I asked people with degrees in linguistics, education, and second language acquisition what I should read, and got a list [1–5]. Then I read the books they recommended, and used AI to summarize the denser ones [7].

What follows is what I took from them: the handful of principles most of that research agrees on, several of which are pretty counter-intuitive.

1. Like Learning to Bike

We have specialized circuits in our brain for acquiring language, and true learning only happens when you engage them.

A tech person would say that the skill of communication is “hardware-accelerated” in our brains. It’s like a GPU in a computer that can only do one thing - graphics - but does it very fast [8].

What are the consequences of that? For one thing, all the heavy lifting in language acquisition is completely subconscious.

It’s akin to learning to ride a bike. It’s about giving your subconscious the time and opportunity to figure things out on its own. Your part is to just keep trying and “getting on the bike” and other than that, get out of the way and trust the process.

Consciously studying about the language (grammar, phonetics) helps about as much as physics lectures help you ride a bike.

That is, not that much, especially for kids and beginners [2, 3, 5].

2. Input Before Output

I think this particular point was the most counter-intuitive and surprising.

Brain studies suggest there is fundamentally only one way we acquire language: by practicing understanding of the input: reading and listening.

Surprisingly, it looks like producing language output —speaking and writing— are not separate skills, but are actually largely powered by the skill of input comprehension as well.

If language acquisition were a house, understanding input is building the foundation, and the ability to generate output is putting up the roof.

That means that spending too much time on output before the foundation of input understanding is built is overrated.

Another aspect of this is that it’s a good idea to hold off on constantly testing and evaluating how well a kid is doing. Soft and squishy biological things like our brains do not have progress bars that need to go to 100%.

One manifestation of this dynamic is that when babies learn their first language, they have a long “silent period” where they understand a great deal but speak very little [2].

This counter-intuitive point is somewhat hard to apply in practice for social reasons: when a person is watching cartoons, for example, and not being barraged with output exercises all the time, it doesn’t look like she’s learning.

She’s just not suffering enough.

3. Words Map to the World

We have family friends whose kids are growing up with three languages. Once, someone who couldn’t quite believe it asked their seven-year-old: “You speak Spanish? Okay, how do you say green in Spanish?”

The boy froze. He couldn’t answer.

Then his older sister pointed at a green cup on the table and asked him, in Spanish, what color it was. “Verde,” he said, instantly.

He knew the word perfectly well. It just wasn’t where the question was looking for it. In his head, verde was attached to the color itself, not to the English word “green.” The person asking assumed a language is a lookup table: a list of foreign words, each paired with a “real” word in your own language. That’s how adults are usually taught languages. It’s not how the language instinct works.

The difference matters because the two kinds of link are made by very different parts of the brain.

Translating is an intellectual puzzle: convert one sequence of symbols into another. That’s a job for conscious reasoning, the prefrontal cortex. And conscious reasoning has very little bandwidth. It’s slow, it’s already busy with everything else, and in kids it’s still years from being fully grown.

The language instinct is a different beast. It’s older, deeper, and enormously powerful; it absorbs words and grammar faster than any amount of studying could. But it does one particular thing: it connects words to things and ideas in the world, and to what other people mean when they use them. To the instinct, a language is a tool for understanding people, not a set of correspondences to memorize.

Now spin up Duolingo, or walk into a school French class.

“Hier il pleuvait, mais aujourd’hui il fait beau.”

- Oh, so it was raining? Where?

- Nowhere in particular. It’s just an example.

- So it’s make-believe? A lie?

- No. It’s much, much less than that.

Nobody gets wet. Nobody cares about the weather. The point of the sentence is to turn it into “Yesterday it was raining, but today it’s nice out.” So il pleuvait gets wired to the English words, and never to water falling from the sky.

That’s not just inefficient. It trains the wrong habit. Every exercise like this teaches the brain that French isn’t something people use to talk about the world. It’s a strange puzzle you solve to please adults. Classrooms send the same message another way: the moment the teacher needs to say something that actually matters, she switches to English [6].

Imagine Professor Pavlov devising another cruel experiment: conditioning his poor dogs to get bored and zone out at the first sound of French. How would he do it?

He would flood them with sentences that mean nothing in particular.

4. Stories Make It Real

Suppose you accept all that. Then what? If la pluie has to connect to real rain, does every learner need to move to France and stand outside in it?

That sounds expensive. And a cartoon doesn’t seem any better than Duolingo here. When a character on screen says “Il pleut !”, nobody in your living room gets wet either.

But the language instinct has two allies, both about as old as it is: the instinct for stories and the instinct for play.

When we follow a story, the rain in it is almost as real to us as rain outside. We feel the chill, we worry the hero will get soaked, we want to know what happens next. That’s enough. The word attaches to the experience, not to a translation.

Play works the same way. In a game, words are tools for doing something you want: winning, figuring something out, getting the joke.

This is no accident. Stories and play are how kids learn about the world on their own. It’s why they would rather play than sit through a lesson, and why they ask for the same story again and again. The tragedy of the classroom isn’t that it asks kids to learn. It’s that it ignores the way they already know how to.

So the line that matters isn’t between a classroom and a trip to France. It’s between translating a sentence about rain and following a story where it rains. Both use the same French words. Only one reaches the part of the brain that acquires language.

5. It’s Okay to Be Confused

In a class, not understanding every word is treated like a failure.

But in reality, guessing from context and “winging it” is a core part of language acquisition.

We do it constantly in our native language too, mostly unconsciously.

Getting comfortable with ambiguity is part of the process. The impulse to stop and dissect every sentence you don’t fully understand is counterproductive.

6. Use the Real Thing, not Book Language

It’s often pointed out that naturally spoken French is so different from textbook French that it’s reasonable to treat them as two separate languages:

  • Spoken French. The language millions of people use to communicate.
  • Textbook French. A dead language for books and exercises. Like Latin.

Which one’s worth it? The answer is obvious.

And yes, if you just take any random utterance from the street said by one native to another in their natural habitat of, say, France or Quebec, it won’t be good learning material.

But that’s true for any language:

Language used in kids’ TV shows is one example of great learning material to practice that real deal: Spoken French.

When kids’ TV shows are produced, millions of dollars are at stake. Hundreds of people work on a show for years. People’s careers and mortgages are on the line.

You can be sure they’ve made certain the show is understandable to French-speaking children of various ages and levels of language mastery.

The cinematography and voice acting are often brilliant, memorable and impactful.

Why not use those?

Still, it cannot be that one-sided, right? What does Textbook French have going for it?

Most arguments about the importance of Textbook French are weak. They sound like arguments for learning Latin. We get to hear arguments involving people who died a long time ago. We are told that it’s how things have always been. We hear that the “ne” in “Je ne sais pas” must live there forever because it comes from Latin negation. And that is why those who mumble “Ch’pas” instead are despicable, uncultured lowlifes we all must look down upon.

If you cast all this garbage aside, the only potentially solid argument is that Spoken French, like any other purely oral language, doesn’t have a proper writing system that serves its needs.

But let’s be honest, no form of French does.

Frenchirix

With all that in mind, I set up my kids’ extra French practice around what the research says.

After some trial and error, we settled on an approach that seems to work pretty well. It wasn’t Duolingo. It has two parts:

  • regular exposure to French media the kids enjoy and pick themselves,
  • spaced repetition of that same content [4].

Altogether it takes only 10–15 minutes a day. But if it’s regular, the effect is outsized.

The first part was easy. Nobody complained about the extra screen time.

The second part was hard. To do spaced repetition right, you need to:

  • keep track of what you reviewed and when,
  • have some excuse to go through the dialogue again, word by word,

…and do it all without forcing it on the kid too much.

I couldn’t find a good tool for this, so I started building one. It grew into a small side project called Frenchirix. The name is a spoof of Asterix (or, if you’re their copyright lawyer, of ancient Gallic names [9]).

The main idea is simple: use the French version of a TV show your kid already loves. When a kid loves a show, emotions do the heavy lifting. Laughing at a joke or feeling for a character opens a gate, and while it’s open, the French dialogue slips in almost unnoticed. It’s no longer a string of foreign words; it’s part of something the kid already cares about.

Right now, the app uses dialogue from Asterix & Obelix: The Big Fight [9], a brilliant French cartoon my kids like.

Here’s how it works:

  • It’s spaced repetition for TV shows. Each session is about five minutes: enough to actually work, not so much that it becomes a chore. The app keeps track of what to review and when.

  • It’s for re-exposure, not for tests. The mini-games are just an excuse to go over the dialogue again. The answers are always on the same page, so if you forget something, you can still finish the exercise; it just takes a few more taps. Kids never get stuck and frustrated. The point is another exposure, not passing a quiz.

  • I’m building it for my kids first. That means I get daily, honest feedback. Many ideas I thought were clever turned out not to work, and vice versa. That daily reality check keeps the project grounded.

  • It does one thing: practice understanding real spoken French. Frenchirix is only for comprehension of real, native media. Doing that one thing well is the whole goal.

That’s pretty much it. I’m trying to apply what the science says works, not what people expect language learning to look like.

The code is open source. It’s on GitHub as frenchirix-open-source, under the MIT license. It has two parts:

  • A pipeline that turns your own video files into practice material. Point it at, say, the episodes of a TV show: it picks out short dialogue scenes, transcribes them, and aligns every word to the audio, so any word or expression can be replayed on its own.

  • The web app itself, with the practice puzzles and the spaced repetition.

It doesn’t come with any show’s material, so you build the content from videos you have. It was built for French learned through English, but the approach works for any language pair. It’s a working project rather than a polished product, so expect to read the docs and change some code.

That’s all, folks!

There you have it, my understanding of academic science about language learning, and how I am trying to apply this to my little side project called Frenchirix.
